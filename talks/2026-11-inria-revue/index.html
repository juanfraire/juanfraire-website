[00:00–00:20] Good morning, and thank you, Oana, Maxime and Christelle, for the invitation. I am Juan Fraire, from the Agora team in Lyon. Today: how satellites are turning into computers, what we have contributed, and where the limits lie.

[00:20–01:05] Satellites are becoming computers. A communication satellite used to be a bent pipe: receive, amplify, send back down. Then payloads became regenerative, and with 5G non-terrestrial networks they can host a base station on board. [point to the green dot] In November 2025, Starcloud-1 flew an NVIDIA H100 and ran a language model in orbit. [sweep right] The filings followed: SpaceX asked for up to a million satellites, Blue Origin for fifty-one thousand, and Google presented Suncatcher [point to the orange dot]. From one GPU to a million satellites. The pitch: abundant sunlight, no cooling water, no land. Whether it holds is where I will end.

[01:05–01:10] The plan: foundations, then our two research axes, then the limits. Foundations first: networks that move.

[01:10–01:55] [point to the panels] Satellite networks come in a few architectures. A sparse constellation stores data and carries it to the next ground station. IoT devices reach a satellite directly or through a gateway. A dense mesh routes in orbit over laser links. Three facts hold for all of them. [point to Moving] The nodes move: seven and a half kilometres per second, a few minutes over any point. [point to Predictable] They move predictably: orbital mechanics gives every contact in advance, so we can route on a schedule. [point to Scarce] And everything is scarce: energy in eclipse, contact time, antennas, mass. Moving, predictable, scarce: these three words carry through the talk.

[01:55–02:25] [point left] Our team works along two axes. AI for Space: intelligence that operates space systems, from fleet operations to access and routing. An established field. [click] Space for AI: orbital infrastructure that hosts computing. [point to the magenta bar] Here industry has launch cadence and huge filings, and almost no science: how to design, operate and assess these systems before committing hundreds of billions? [click] Where the two meet: the space cloud, built on connectivity, dependability and sustainability.

[02:25–02:30] First axis: AI for Space. Where does learning actually help run satellite networks?

[02:30–03:15] In the ConstellAI project for ESA, with GMV, Thales Alenia Space and Saarland, we asked exactly that. Take routing. [point to the path] Traffic crosses inter-satellite links, and we want the lowest end-to-end latency. [point to the equation] It has two parts. Propagation follows from orbital mechanics: predictable, so we compute it. Queueing depends on traffic: uncertain, so we learn it. [point to the update rule] Q-routing does that: each node keeps the expected delay through each neighbour and corrects it after every hop. [click] It beats Dijkstra by up to four milliseconds, and when nodes fail, it keeps the network connected. Compute what the orbits predict; learn what they hide.

[03:15–03:55] [point to the taped figure] The second use case: scheduling acquisitions and downlinks for fifty to a hundred satellites, without draining batteries or overflowing memory. [point to the top panel] Reinforcement learning learns a policy by trial and error. [point to the bottom panel] Simulated annealing searches the plan itself, sometimes keeping a worse plan to escape a local optimum. [click] Annealing won: up to fifteen percent more reward, with half the variance. [click] The rule: with a known model and a large schedule, optimize; when the environment shifts, learn. And in orbit, most of the model is known. In most cases, optimization beats learning.

[03:55–04:40] [point to the bar] AI is also becoming a payload. Earth-observation satellites generate about 5.2 petabytes a day, and only 2.3 come down: 57 percent never reaches the ground. So we bring the computation to the data. [click] Train in orbit: with federated learning, only model updates travel. In FedSat-LAM, with UC Berkeley, each satellite fine-tunes 0.13 percent of a 632-million-parameter model, aggregated within and across orbital planes, with 95 percent less energy per round than a standard baseline. [click] Infer in orbit: drop the cloudy image and the empty sea, as ESA's Φ-sat-1 did. [click] Either way, computation, communication and energy must be designed together.

> AI is now **operator and payload**: either way, demand for computing moves to orbit.

[04:40–05:25] Working with Safran and Loft Orbital on embedded AI, three open problems came out. [point to the first card] First, debugging a model you cannot reach: if it only sends down positives, you never measure what it misses, so the downlink must be designed as an experiment. [click] Second, energy-aware inference. [point to the discs] The share of each orbit spent in shadow depends on the altitude and on beta, the angle between the orbit plane and the Sun. [point to the timeline] A scheduler can run a large model in sunlight and a small one in eclipse. [click] Third, swapping models in flight, safely. Either way, AI creates demand for computing in orbit.

[05:25–05:35] Second axis: Space for AI. Instead of using AI to run satellites, we use satellites to run AI. How should that infrastructure be built?

[05:35–06:05] With Wenxiao Ge and Kanglian Zhao in Nanjing, we proposed a reference architecture for space cloud networks. [point to the layers] A cloud layer of space-based data centres on optical links, an access layer connecting users to them, and the user layer on the ground. Two design choices matter: where the data centres orbit, and whether they are embedded in the access constellation or fly on their own.

[06:05–06:35] We simulated both, with users spread over the globe. [click] Embedded in Starlink's first shell, 48 data centres put about 90 percent of users within 12 milliseconds. [click] As an independent dawn-dusk constellation, every user stays within 40 milliseconds. [point to the bottom lines] Close to users means a mid-inclined orbit, a third of it in Earth's shadow: batteries, hence mass. Always in the sun means further from users. This trade-off returns with the limits.

[06:35–07:10] The next step is to rehearse operation before anything flies. With Camilo Rojas, Fabio Patrone and Mario Marchese in Genoa, we built MeteorNet. [walk the stack from the top] Orbits propagated with SGP4, links computed at every step, the constellation emulated as a real network with an SDN controller, real edge services in containers on top, and telemetry for every event. Simulators give the dynamics without the stack; testbeds give the stack without the dynamics. MeteorNet gives both, in continuous time, and it is open source.

[07:10–07:30] [follow the red numbers] One task, end to end: devices send it through the satellite in view, across inter-satellite links, to a satellite whose edge server is on, and the result must come back within the deadline. On the ground, the operations centre runs the SDN controller and the orchestrator.

[07:30–08:20] Three questions anyone running a space cloud will face. [point to the first plot] When to switch a server on? On a ring of ten satellites with a 2.5-second deadline, the controllers run about half the servers at light load. But about 80 percent of tasks fail whatever the policy: no satellite in view in time. The bottleneck is visibility, not compute, and only coupling the orbits with the real stack shows it. [click] How much load per server: serve here, pass to a neighbour, or send down, as visibility and battery change. [click] And where: with 198 satellites, ground servers win up to four; from six on, servers in orbit win.

[08:20–08:30] So, the limits. So far, how to build and run a space cloud. Now the harder question: what does it cost, and is the cost justified?

[08:30–09:30] Let me walk you through our lifecycle audit of orbital computing. First, the pitch. Data centres used about 415 terawatt-hours in 2024, and the IEA expects that to more than double by 2030. In orbit, a panel receives 1,361 watts per square metre, four times the Earth's average, and a dawn-dusk orbit sees the sun over 99 percent of the time. No cooling water, no land, no grid queue. Our models credit every one of these advantages. [click] But an advantage is one line on a bill. The whole bill is carbon accounting: the embodied carbon of every kilogram, built, launched and brought back down, plus operation, divided by the computing actually delivered. Our models, ESpaS and DepTax, are open: every number in this part can be recomputed.

[09:30–11:10] First, the carbon of getting there. Everyone expects the rocket to be the problem: launching a kilogram costs about 36 kilograms of CO2-equivalent on Falcon 9 and 21 on Starship, reuse and propellant counted. Substantial, and payable. [point to the orange] The step nobody prices is coming back down. A deorbited satellite ablates, and part of it becomes nitrogen oxides in the upper atmosphere. At a twenty-year warming potential of 33, re-entry adds 46 to 62 percent on top of the launch: 13 kilograms per kilogram on Starship, 16 on Falcon 9, which also burns its upper stage. And the rules push fleets to burn: the FCC wants low-orbit satellites disposed of within five years after the mission. A graveyard orbit avoids the toll, and leaves a dead satellite behind for centuries. [click] Then, "energy is free". The sun is, but eclipses need batteries. Orbital electricity comes out at 52 grams per kilowatt-hour on Starship and 66 on Falcon 9, against 34 for solar plus storage on the ground: half again to twice as dirty. [point to the dark marks] Those marks are the panels alone: six to eight grams. Almost the whole bill is the battery. That is why the filings love dawn-dusk orbits, with near-permanent sunlight and no battery. Keep that escape route in mind; it comes back with its own price.

[11:10–12:25] Second, the mass of staying alive. [point to the waffle] Take a one-kilowatt, single-GPU node; each square is one percent of launched mass. Half is radiator, a quarter is battery, and the computer is the five blue squares. The radiator alone weighs more than twelve times the GPU it cools. [click] Why? Space has no convection. Every watt the chip dissipates must leave as radiation, and Stefan–Boltzmann sets the price: emissivity, area, and the fourth power of the panel's temperature, minus that of what it faces, cold space and a warm Earth. And the panel can never run hotter than the chip, about 80 degrees. We calibrate on the Space Station's early thermal system: 42 square metres for 14 kilowatts. [point to the bars] Hotter or lighter panels help, but in every case we computed, the radiator still outweighs the GPU at least three times. That is also why PUE, facility power over IT power, misleads up there: it looks perfect, because the cost has moved into launched mass.

[12:25–13:45] So when does a one-kilowatt node pay back its launch? [point to the blue curve] Against the global-average data centre, within about two years: the headline number, against a low bar. Against a green data centre, on the Finnish grid at 68 grams per kilowatt-hour with a PUE of 1.2, parity comes around year nine on Starship, and not within a decade on Falcon 9. [point to the steps] The staircase is reliability. Commercial GPUs are not built for radiation: the dose accumulates, and the sparse public test data suggest a GPU dies after about three years. So a mission of T years flies T over three GPUs, rounded up, the spares kept cold, and every step is one more GPU on the manifest. [point to the dotted line] The dotted curve is the brochure: no spares, a mission flown by a GPU that died in year three. [point to green and orange] The two escape routes in the filings, a battery-free dawn-dusk orbit or a graveyard instead of re-entry, pull parity to about year six. Each moves part of the bill elsewhere: to scarce orbits and more radiation, or to a dead satellite parked for centuries.

[13:45–15:25] And one property no terrestrial data centre pays for: orbit has no technicians. On Earth, a failed disk is an operational event, swapped at two in the morning. In orbit it is a design event, paid in mass before launch: whatever redundancy you launch is all you will ever have. [point to the chart] What matters is the gap between expecting to survive and guaranteeing it. A node that expects two GPU failures survives with two spares only 68 percent of the time; guaranteeing 95 percent takes five. Certainty is bought at the tail. [click] Here is the price, for a one-kilowatt node over five years. Nobody has published a failure rate for this hardware, so we span a factor of ten. [point to the black dots] The black dots are the carbon-optimal designs. A spare adds about 31 percent carbon on paper, yet at higher failure rates it pays for itself: the node delivers more work before it dies. [point to the diamonds] The magenta diamonds are the guarantee: one chance in a thousand of failing. That costs 77 to 86 percent more carbon per delivered GPU-hour, and between 5 and 14 copies, depending on a rate we do not know. At the high end, the node emits more than an average terrestrial data centre. [point to the green card] Spares on board still beat launching replacements, about three times greener. Orbital data centres will be born over-provisioned.

[15:25–15:35] Let me close with a research agenda: computing without repair. The limits I just showed open scientific problems for our community.

[15:35–16:20] [point to the loop] First, the environment is endogenous. Remember the loop on the title slide? This one works against us. Redundancy is bought with mass. Mass adds cross-section and, eventually, debris. Debris raises the collision hazard, which raises every failure rate, which demands more redundancy. [click] And failures in orbit are correlated: in February 2022, one geomagnetic storm brought down 38 of 49 freshly launched Starlink satellites. So the redundancy depends on the hazard, and the hazard on the redundancy. A consistent design survives the environment it helps create: a fixed point. When none exists, the design collapses under its own debris.

[16:20–17:05] Put together, the costs of one node and this loop turn a yes-or-no question into a region. A design fixes the orbit, the mission duration, the redundancy, the disposal and the application. It is feasible when it survives with probability one minus epsilon, beats the terrestrial carbon intensity, and stays within a footprint budget. [point left] Under one set of assumptions, there is a window. [point right] Make radiation harsher and launch dirtier, and it closes. [click] Dependable or sustainable: orbital computing may not get both. That region can be empty. Mapping it, and certifying it, is the science I would like to build with you.

[17:05–17:35] To wrap up. The space cloud sits where AI for Space meets Space for AI: satellites are now operated with AI, and they host it. And the open challenge: orbital computing is flying ahead of its own science. Nobody can yet say whether a data centre no one will ever repair can be both dependable and sustainable. That science should exist before the fleets do.
